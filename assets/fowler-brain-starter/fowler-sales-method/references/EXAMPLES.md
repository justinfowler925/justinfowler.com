# Annotated synthetic examples

Version: 0.1.0 · Every organization and person below is fictional. These fixtures contain no product capability claims.

## Input fixture

F1 — Fictional Harborlight Services public announcement, 2026-09-01: “We plan to open two regional service offices next year.”

F2 — Fictional discovery notes, 2026-09-08. Morgan, operations lead: “New locations make onboarding consistency harder. I want to understand approaches other teams use. There is no approved project budget yet.” Morgan agreed to provide a current onboarding checklist; no delivery date was agreed. Our salesperson offered to send five discovery questions. No pilot, purchase, or executive introduction was agreed.

F3 — Fictional meeting invitation, 2026-09-15: follow-up discussion with Morgan on onboarding consistency. No other attendee is listed.

No approved product specification, pricing schedule, CRM connection, or financial baseline is supplied.

## Acceptable brief excerpt

Decision to advance: determine whether onboarding consistency is a problem worth formally scoping with Morgan.

Verified public context: Harborlight announced plans for two regional offices next year [F1, 2026-09-01]. This is an announcement of intent, not proof the offices have opened.

Buyer statement: Morgan described onboarding consistency as a concern and said there is no approved project budget [F2, 2026-09-08].

Hypothesis: variation between locations may create rework. Alternative: the issue may be missing documentation rather than a need for new technology.

Question: “Could you walk me through one recent onboarding inconsistency and what happened next?”

Unknowns: frequency, measurable impact, decision authority, solution fit, and funding process.

Proposed next step: review the current checklist together and decide whether a defined assessment would be useful. Checklist delivery date remains unagreed.

Why this passes: source labels support the claims, the public announcement remains a plan, buyer interest is not treated as qualified demand, and the next action can reduce uncertainty.

## Unacceptable brief excerpt

“Harborlight is opening two offices and urgently needs our solution. Morgan is the champion and will secure the budget for a September pilot.”

Why this fails: it converts a plan into certainty, invents urgency and product fit, assigns an unsupported champion role, contradicts the budget statement, and invents a pilot and deadline.

## Acceptable follow-up draft

Subject: Questions for our onboarding discussion

Morgan,

You mentioned that keeping onboarding consistent becomes harder as new locations are added. For our next conversation, I suggest we focus on where the process varies and what that variation causes.

As promised, here are five questions to guide the discussion:

1. Where does onboarding most often differ between locations?
2. What happened in the most recent example?
3. How do you currently recognize and correct an inconsistency?
4. Who else helps define the onboarding process?
5. What would make an assessment useful enough to prioritize?

You offered to share the current checklist. Would reviewing it together on September 15 be useful?

Internal review notes: F2 supports the concern and checklist commitment; F3 supports the meeting date. This is an unsent draft. No product promise or pilot commitment is included. Confirm recipient details before any authorized send.
