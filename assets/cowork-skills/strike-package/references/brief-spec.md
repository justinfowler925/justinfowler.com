# Strike Package Brief Specification

Use this as an adaptable document pattern, not a checklist that forces unsupported content.

## Cover block

- User or client organization name, if supplied
- `Pre-Meeting Intelligence Brief`
- Subject name
- Verified current title and organization, with citation
- Meeting date, if known
- Prepared date and research cutoff date
- Handling or distribution label only if the user requests or supplies it

Do not invent a confidentiality marking, distribution list, meeting date, or client branding.

## Executive summary

Open with a short bottom line: who the person is, why the meeting may matter, the strongest evidence-backed meeting objective, and the most important evidence gap. Mark strategic conclusions as assessments.

## 1. Subject Profile

Use a compact two-column table. Include only verified and relevant fields, such as current role, documented remit, prior roles, education or credentials, public professional focus, regional or sector experience, and attributable public priorities. Put citations in each value cell.

Do not infer network membership, authority, influence, shared ground, or access from mere institutional proximity.

## 2. Why This Meeting Matters

Explain the strategic relevance using cited facts and clearly labeled assessments. Separate:

- verified responsibilities or priorities;
- the user’s stated objective and capabilities; and
- the resulting assessment of possible value.

State what a realistic outcome looks like. Avoid declaring that the subject is or is not a buyer, gatekeeper, decision-maker, or sponsor unless a source directly supports that authority.

## 3. Opportunity Mapping

Organize two to five opportunity areas by documented need, initiative, responsibility, or operational context. For each include:

- **Evidence:** verified facts with citations;
- **Relevance:** a labeled assessment connecting evidence to the user’s offering;
- **Question to test:** an open meeting question; and
- **Confidence:** high, medium, or low, based on evidence quality and fit.

Do not state a problem exists merely because the organization’s sector could experience it.

## 4. Meeting Strategy

Include an opening approach, priority topics, questions, signals to listen for, and cautions. Base rapport only on public professional interests or user-confirmed shared experience. Phrase suggested language naturally and avoid manipulation.

A two-column `Evidence of traction / Neutral or deferring signal` table may be useful, but present it as interpretation guidance rather than a prediction about the person.

## 5. Organization and Operating Context

Summarize only the current business, policy, regulatory, or institutional conditions that materially affect the meeting. Cite each bullet or paragraph. Distinguish enacted rules from proposals and current programs from historical events.

## 6. Alternatives and Competitive Context

Include this section only when relevant and support every vendor, product, capability, and market claim. Use a table such as `Alternative / Verified capability / Possible differentiation / Source`. Treat differentiation as an assessment unless independently substantiated.

## 7. Proposed Next Steps

Offer a small set of proportional outcomes, typically best, good, and minimum. Tie each to the meeting objective and do not assume the subject will make introductions, share confidential information, or bypass procurement. A useful next step is specific, ethical, and within the subject’s plausible documented remit.

## 8. Risks and Mitigations

Use a table: `Risk / Evidence or rationale / Likelihood / Mitigation`. Label likelihood as an assessment. Include evidence gaps, identity ambiguity, stale information, authority uncertainty, regulatory constraints, and reputational or privacy issues when material.

## 9. Sources and Verification Notes

Provide the numbered source register described in `research-standards.md`, followed by:

- research cutoff date;
- unresolved contradictions;
- client-provided claims not independently verified;
- LinkedIn surfaces and date ranges reviewed, source access classes, and material audience restrictions;
- inaccessible or weak sources that affected confidence; and
- omitted sections or facts and why they were omitted.

## Document presentation

Match the examples’ professional briefing style without copying their person-specific content:

- clear title page or cover block;
- restrained organizational branding supplied by the user;
- numbered major sections and concise subsection headings;
- compact profile, signal, competitor, and risk tables;
- short paragraphs and bullets suited to pre-meeting scanning;
- consistent header/footer and page numbering; and
- working clickable citations plus a complete source register.

Favor legibility over density. Prevent split table headers, orphaned headings, clipped URLs, and unreadably small citation text. Visually inspect the rendered document before delivery.
