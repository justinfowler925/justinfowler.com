The first forest I built was bad enough that I pulled it from my website.

The render had completed. The file played. I still would not use it.

That gap became the starting point for Hollywood, my audio, video and broadcasting skill—and the production agent behind it.

I originally wanted to talk to my computer and have it make useful things. Music. Nature sounds. An ocean view that could keep playing while the next piece was being made.

The early videos kept restarting their camera moves. The stationary ocean barely felt alive. Adding more effects to a rough forest made it a busier rough forest.

The change was to work on the entire production path: stronger source scenes, guided motion, a small rotating library, and audio that keeps its own clock.

Then I tested the interruptions.

Kill the playback worker. Restart the service. Keep the GPU busy. Submit the same request 24 times. Check whether the soundtrack resets and whether the preview files keep piling up.

The current build passed 45 automated tests and a one-hour private broadcast test. Batching and tiled decoding brought measured renderer allocation down from about 65.5 GiB to 24–27 GiB in four-second comparisons.

It still takes minutes to generate seconds of footage. The useful answer is a compact library that can cover the wait, with clear limits on what is actually live and what is repeating.

I wrote up the journey, including the failed approaches, the fixes, and three playable demonstrations: ocean, forest and a fireplace cabin.

Hollywood is my orchestration and production workflow, built with public engines, models and assets whose authors deserve their credit. It is a developing studio, and the results are there to inspect.
